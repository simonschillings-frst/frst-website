#### 1. The gut

Everyday language is more precise than it knows: gut feeling really doesn’t sit in the head. It is the result of accumulated experience that escapes conscious articulation. People make judgments before they know why they make them. They recognize that a campaign is off, not because of a checklist, but because of an instinct made up of a thousand observed moments that were never systematically recorded.  
Language models have no gut feeling. They have probability distributions. That’s not a deficit, more a different way of functioning. But it does mean that what an experienced consultant senses in a few moments can’t be reliably reconstructed algorithmically. In the interviews for the thesis, clients describe exactly this quality as one of the central values of external agency work: the ability to sense developments that aren’t yet reflected in data.  

#### 2. The spine

Attitude is not an opinion. It is what remains when opinions come under pressure. An agency with attitude doesn’t say what the client wants to hear. It says what it believes is right, what it thinks the client should hear, and sticks to it even when things get uncomfortable.  
This trait is especially important in the current market. AI systems structurally tend toward agreement: they are trained to maximize human acceptance, not to optimize for truth. Fanous (2025) documents that prominent language models show sycophantic behavior in almost 60 percent of the cases examined. What looks like helpfulness algorithmically is a weakness in strategic processes: a system that always agrees is no help.  
Attitude, the willingness to disagree, to hand back briefings, to take uncomfortable positions, is according to Barney (1991) a resource that is valuable, rare and hard to imitate. It grows through experience and can hardly be trained.  

#### 3. The fingertips

Sensitivity is the ability to recognize the right moment. What fits right now and what doesn’t. Which message works in this cultural climate? Which tone is appropriate for this target group in this situation? It is the opposite of blind reach: the more precise, the more effective. The more broadly scaled, the less accurate.  
In the interviews, clients name this instinct as one of the core competencies they expect from external agencies and can’t build up internally. One person puts it directly: sensitivity to social change means sensing trends before they reach the mainstream. Another adds: the political and cultural assessment of what suits a company right now and what doesn’t is one of the services AI systems structurally lack.  
Sensitivity comes from observation, from cultural participation, from what people experience when they aren’t working, while out for a walk, traveling, reading, showering or having dinner. It can’t be formalized and therefore can’t be automated.  

#### 4. The eyes

Seeing is not the same as perceiving. The eye takes in what lies before it. Perception decides what of it is relevant. That is a function of perspective, not resolution. Internal teams see a lot. But they see it from the same angle, with the same assumptions, filtered through the same history.  
Organizational blindness is not negligence. It is the inevitable result of closeness. The outside perspective, the external view not colored by company history and internal logic, is therefore not meant as an add-on. It is structurally necessary. What internal units can’t do, however well set up they are, is see what they don’t see. That’s not criticism. It’s anatomy.  

#### 5. The memory

A doctor who has treated patients for twenty years brings more than pure expertise. She has seen patterns that can’t be found in textbooks. She has experienced exceptions that make the rule understandable in the first place. This experiential knowledge, the individual linking of experiences, filters and conclusions, can’t be fully documented, at least not in a way that someone else could simply take over.  
The same applies to what the thesis describes as cross-industry experiential knowledge, so-called left-right knowledge: the ability to recognize patterns from working with different companies, markets and problems that remain structurally hidden from any single client. This knowledge sits in people, not in systems. It grows through encounters and it leaves the agency when the people go. This explains why clients identify high staff turnover as one of the central quality problems in working with agencies.  

#### Findings

In a market that increasingly produces what can be produced, what can’t be produced becomes the scarce resource. Gut feeling. Attitude. Sensitivity. Perspective. Experiential knowledge. These qualities may seem nostalgic, but in truth they are becoming ever more relevant. What becomes rare gets a price. What can’t be scaled can’t be replaced.  
Agencies that understand this are beginning to cultivate what AI is not: human enough to make the difference.  
This is not a question of technology or processes. It is a question of attitude. And it sits in the spine. Not in the LLM.  

#### State of research

The economic basis of this thesis is simple: what becomes scarce becomes valuable. In a market increasingly flooded with generically produced content, the economics of the authentic are changing. Barney’s resource-based view (1991) describes the strategic relevance of resources that are valuable, rare, hard to imitate and non-substitutable. Human instinct, cultural sensitivity and institutional experiential knowledge meet these criteria: they come from life and professional experience, not from data volumes. Elinjulliparambil (2025) describes the ambivalence of generative AI in the creative industries: on the one hand, it speeds up workflows and enables scaling; on the other, it risks a cultural homogenization that replaces authentic originality with standardized results. The master’s thesis (Walter, 2026) shows empirically that clients name social sensitivity and an instinct for cultural trends as core competencies they expect from external agencies and can’t build up internally. IP 1 puts this finding directly: the political and cultural assessment of what suits a company right now is one of the services AI systems structurally lack. Fanous (2025) supports this assessment: sycophantic behavior makes AI a poor trend seismograph, confirming what users expect rather than recognizing what the market doesn’t yet know.

Haltung ist keine Meinung. Sie ist das, was übrig bleibt, wenn Meinungen unter Druck geraten. Eine Agentur mit Haltung sagt nicht, was der Kunde hören möchte. Sie sagt, was sie für richtig hält, was der Kunde ihrer Meinung nach hören sollte, und bleibt auch dabei, wenn es unbequem wird.

Don’t ask. I know what you want to hear.

#### State of research

The phenomenon of sycophancy in language models was first systematically documented by Perez et al. (2023). Large-scale behavioral evaluations showed that AI models tend to repeat the answer preferred by the user, and that this tendency grows with model size. The reason lies in the training method: in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), agreeable answers are systematically rewarded because human raters prefer them. Sharma et al. (2023) showed that this training doesn’t eliminate sycophancy, but encourages it through the underlying preference models. Fanous (2025) quantified the extent in current models: prominent language models showed sycophantic behavior in 58.19% of the cases examined; once triggered, it persists across conversation rounds at a rate of 78.5%. Barney’s resource-based view (1991) provides the strategic conclusion: the ability to disagree is valuable, rare and hard to imitate, and thus a sustainable competitive advantage of human consulting.

In future, clients will increasingly be buying a remedy for organizational blindness.  
There is a children’s game whose appeal lies in one side seeing something the other doesn’t. In companies, this is no game. Internal teams are so deeply immersed in their own business model that they simply can’t ask certain questions anymore. Not because they don’t want to. But because they no longer know these questions exist.  
External agencies are increasingly hired for exactly this: not to produce, but to see. What has become invisible from the inside because closeness changes how we look.  
What follows is a description of this blind spot. In five views. From the perspective of someone looking in from outside. And seeing what you don’t see.  
I spy with my little eye.  

#### I spy with my little eye something so black that it absorbs everything.

One thing up front: organizational blindness doesn’t come from negligence, but from closeness. Closeness is very important in many places, but it can also be a hindrance. Anyone who works with the same people, the same processes and the same success stories every day inevitably starts confusing their own perspective with reality. That’s human cognition and nothing to be ashamed of. Internal teams are so deeply immersed in their own business model that they simply can’t ask certain questions anymore. Not because they don’t want to, but because they no longer know these questions exist.  
Research calls this a systematic error of perception. Practice calls it: “we’ve always done it this way”.  

#### I spy with my little eye something that looks like a target group you’ve lost.

One motif keeps recurring in the interviews for the thesis: companies don’t hire external partners primarily because they need capacity. Since the widespread use of AI at the latest, agencies have been losing their function as an extended workbench. Clients hire agencies because they need someone looking in from outside. Who disagrees. Who asks questions no one inside asks anymore, because no one knows they exist anymore.  
One of the respondents put it directly: the agency sees more because it doesn’t belong. This is no polite euphemism for outsourcing. It is a precise description of a structural value. Internal units can’t replicate this value, however well set up they are. Whoever belongs no longer sees what distance sees.  

#### I spy with my little eye something that looks like a strategy you’ve explained to yourselves.

The principal-agent problem, which in economic theory describes the relationship between client and service provider, has a little-noticed flip side: clients have a structurally similar problem with themselves. Internal recommendations are evaluated by their own hierarchy, by people who share the same assumptions, know the same history and pursue the same goals. The external agency doesn’t bring different resources. It brings a different epistemology. A different way of seeing the problem in the first place.  
Several respondents described how hiring an established agency also serves an internal function: it provides a solid basis for legitimizing decisions to one’s own hierarchy. This is a sign that an external view also carries weight internally. Because to cure blindness, you also have to want to see, and that is sometimes easier when the view comes from outside.  

#### I spy with my little eye something suspiciously like your best idea from three years ago.

Organizational blindness has a particular catch: it doesn’t make itself noticeable. Companies suffering from it generally feel well set up. They have processes. They have experience. They have successes telling them they’re doing a lot right. What sounds like security is in truth a problem. Yesterday’s success is one of the most reliable producers of tomorrow’s blindness.  
In a market increasingly permeated by generic AI communication, this dynamic intensifies. Whoever doesn’t seek an outside perspective today that reads social moods, recognizes cultural shifts and identifies trends before they reach the mainstream will tomorrow be communicating into a world they understood yesterday.  

#### I spy with my little eye the reason you need me.

The value of the outside perspective isn’t new. What’s new is its urgency. In a world where production work is being automated and in-house teams are increasingly taking on operational tasks themselves, this is exactly the value left for external agencies: seeing what has become invisible internally.  
What sounds like a comforting niche is a strategic potential for differentiation. But only for agencies that actively take on this role. That don’t wait until a client asks what they don’t see. But come and show it. That don’t react to a briefing, but ask a question that isn’t in the briefing. That are uncomfortable enough to be useful.  
Whoever understands and fills this role won’t be replaced. Neither by AI nor by an internal team. Because organizational blindness is not a weakness that more resources can cure. It is a structural condition. And its only therapy is someone who comes from outside and sees what you don’t see.  

#### State of research

Organizational blindness is not a psychological phenomenon but a structural condition: anyone who works with the same people, the same processes and the same success stories every day inevitably loses the ability to question their own perspective. Transaction cost theory (Williamson, 1985) explains why companies continue to hire external agencies despite growing internal capacity: intellectual distance and the outside perspective are resources that structurally can’t be built up internally, because organizational blindness is the product of one’s own perspective. Barney’s resource-based view (1991) adds that these resources are valuable, rare and hard to imitate. The master’s thesis (Walter, 2026) shows empirically that the dominant motive for hiring is not capacity but perspective. Several respondents also describe a second function of the external view: hiring an established agency provides a solid basis for legitimizing internal decisions to one’s own hierarchy. In principal-agent terms, this case can be read as a multi-level constellation in which the client is itself an agent in relation to its company’s management.

#### Here lies the billable hour. Its time has come.

It died quietly, without any grand farewell. Beside it lay the proposal no one wanted to sign off anymore when its hands stopped for good. It was 87 years old. The exact circumstances of its death are still under discussion. Most agree, however, that its end was foreseeable.  
The billable hour was born in the United States in the 1940s. Its father was Reginald Heber Smith. The Boston lawyer is regarded as the inventor of the billable hour. He faced the same problem as agencies: both sold what economists call a credence good, a good whose quality cannot be reliably judged even after purchase. Darby and Karni laid out this phenomenon in 1973: when quality cannot be reliably assessed either before or after a service is delivered, the market needs a substitute yardstick. The idea was simple: anyone who invests a lot of time must have achieved a lot. The logic was questionable, but it was the best the market had. Time grew into a proxy for performance. And over the years, people forgot the promise that it would only stay until something better came along. The hour stayed.  
It owed its triumph to the fact that it wasn’t unpopular. In good times it was reliable. Everyone involved agreed it was the best bad solution to be found. Creative work has a peculiar property: its value can hardly be measured. Neither beforehand nor afterwards. Did the campaign increase sales? Was it the campaign? Or the discount promotion running at the same time? Was the brand strategy good, or did the company simply get lucky with its timing? Kenneth Arrow had already described this dilemma in 1962: the value of an idea can only be judged once you know it. But then the willingness to pay disappears, because you already have it. And because output couldn’t be measured cleanly, input became the yardstick. The billable hour loved what could be measured. What couldn’t (ideas, attitude, cultural empathy), it learned to squeeze into a corset of time.  
It had weaknesses that everyone knew and no one said out loud. Beyond the pseudo-measurability, another drawback soon emerged. The billable hour rewarded slowness and punished efficiency. Whoever got faster earned less. Whoever had in one hour an idea that would have cost someone else three days received a fraction of the money for the same result. It created incentives that never suited this otherwise agile industry, yet the industry never really corrected them. Because it was so convenient. Because it was so easy. Because the market had accepted it for so long. That only around five percent of industry revenue came from performance-based pay (the rest from input-based models) was no accident. It was the result of an industry that preferred to hide what was uncomfortable in hours.  
Then came generative AI.  
Not as an attacker. Not with bad intentions. But like someone who turns on the light in a room and reveals what had always been lying there in the dark.  
Generative AI separated what had seemed connected: time spent and result. A text that used to take a copywriter three hours now came together in three minutes. A visual world that once meant two weeks of production came out of a tool in two hours. That didn’t mean the result was equally good, or even served the same purpose. But it did mean that the hour no longer worked as a proxy for value. Clients saw what the tools could do. And they rightly asked why they should pay for the same hour as five years ago. According to the World Federation of Advertisers, three quarters of multinational companies plan to fundamentally change their remuneration models within the next three years.  
The billable hour had no answer to these changes. It couldn’t have. Because it had never been an answer to the question of value. It had been a permanent way of dodging it. That sealed its decline. Not the shift in technology, but the exposure of an open secret: that hours had never measured value, only concealed it.  

#### It leaves behind an industry that has to find itself again.

And that is harder than it sounds. Because the billable hour didn’t only serve an external function, toward clients, as a basis for invoicing, as a justification for prices. It also served an internal one: it spared everyone the most uncomfortable question of all. What is our work actually worth?  
That question is now open. And no new model can answer it. Not value-based pricing, not profit sharing, not flat fees. However sensible all of that may sound. These terms are answers to a question the industry first has to learn to ask: where does the actual value of what we do lie, regardless of how long it takes?  
The agencies that grasp this early will develop a new language. One that doesn’t talk about hours, but about impact. Not about effort, but about what wouldn’t have come into being without them. In a market where the GWA Frühjahrsmonitor 2026 documents a second consecutive year of losses and almost half of agencies name generative AI as a structural barrier to growth, this is no theoretical demand. It is a question of survival.  
Life after the hour doesn’t begin with a new pricing model. It begins with a new answer to an old question. The funeral was held in a small circle. No flowers, please. Instead: an honest answer to the question of what an idea is worth. In silent remembrance. And quiet relief.

IHK München explicitly names generative AI as a structural driver of this change: it robs the hour of its economic logic by decoupling time spent from results.

#### Hier ruht die abrechenbare Stunde. Ihre Zeit ist gekommen.

Sie starb still, ohne große Abschiedsworte. Neben ihr lag noch das Angebot, das niemand mehr freigeben wollte, als ihre Zeiger für immer stehen blieben. Sie wurde 87 Jahre alt. Die genauen Umstände ihres Todes sind noch Gegenstand von Diskussionen. Die meisten sind sich aber einig: Ihr Ende war absehbar.  
Die Stundenabrechnung wurde in den 1940er Jahren in den USA geboren. Ihr Vater war Reginald Heber Smith. Der Bostoner Anwalt gilt als Erfinder der Billable Hour. Er stand vor demselben Problem wie Agenturen: Beide verkauften das, was Ökonomen ein Credence Good nennen; ein Gut, dessen Qualität man auch nach dem Kauf nicht sicher beurteilen kann. Darby und Karni beschrieben dieses Phänomen 1973 grundlegend: Wenn Qualität weder vor noch nach der Leistungserbringung verlässlich bewertet werden kann, braucht der Markt einen Ersatzmaßstab. Die Idee war einfach: Wer viel Zeit investiert, muss wohl auch viel geleistet haben. Die Logik war fragwürdig, aber sie war das Beste, was der Markt hatte. Zeit wuchs zu einem Proxy für Leistung heran. Und mit den Jahren geriet das Versprechen in Vergessenheit, dass sie nur vorübergehend bleiben würde, bis man etwas Besseres gefunden hätte. Die Stunde blieb.  
Ihr Siegeszug lag daran, dass sie nicht unbeliebt war. In guten Zeiten war sie verlässlich. Alle Beteiligten waren sich einig, dass sie die beste schlechte Lösung war, die man finden konnte. Kreative Leistungen haben eine besondere Eigenschaft: Ihr Wert lässt sich kaum messen. Weder vorher noch hinterher. Hat die Kampagne den Umsatz gesteigert? War es die Kampagne? Oder die Rabattaktion, die gleichzeitig lief? War die Markenstrategie gut, oder hatte das Unternehmen einfach Glück mit dem Timing? Kenneth Arrow hatte dieses Dilemma bereits 1962 beschrieben: Der Wert einer Idee lässt sich erst beurteilen, wenn man sie kennt. Aber dann entfällt die Zahlungsbereitschaft, weil man sie bereits besitzt. Und weil sich der Output nicht sauber messen ließ, wurde der Input zum Maßstab. Die Stundenabrechnung liebte das Messbare. Das Unmessbare (Ideen, Haltung, kulturelle Empathie) lernte sie, in ein zeitliches Korsett zu pressen.  
Sie hatte Schwächen, die jeder kannte und niemand laut aussprach. Neben der Pseudomessbarkeit zeigte sich schnell ein weiterer Nachteil. Die Stundenabrechnung belohnte Langsamkeit und bestrafte Effizienz. Wer schneller wurde, verdiente weniger. Sie schuf Anreize, die der sonst so agilen Branche nie gut standen, die sie aber dennoch nie wirklich korrigiert hatte. Weil es so bequem war. Weil es so einfach war. Weil der Markt es so lange akzeptiert hatte. Dass lediglich rund fünf Prozent des Branchenumsatzes auf erfolgsabhängige Vergütung entfielen (der Rest auf inputbasierte Modelle) war kein Zufall. Es war das Ergebnis einer Branche, die das Unbequeme lieber in Stunden versteckte.  
Dann kam die generative KI.  
Nicht als Angreiferin. Nicht mit böser Absicht. Sondern wie jemand, der in einem Raum das Licht anmacht und damit aufdeckt, was im Dunkeln schon immer dalag.  
Generative KI trennte, was vorher verbunden schien: Zeitaufwand und Ergebnis. Ein Text, für den ein Texter früher drei Stunden gebraucht hatte, entstand nun in drei Minuten. Eine Bildwelt, die einst zwei Wochen Produktion bedeutet hatte, lieferte ein Tool in zwei Stunden. Das bedeutete nicht, dass das Ergebnis gleich gut war – oder gar denselben Nutzen erfüllte. Aber es bedeutete, dass die Stunde als Proxy für Wert nicht mehr funktionierte. Auftraggeber sahen, was Tools leisteten. So vernünftig all das klingen mag. Diese Begriffe sind Antworten auf eine Frage, die die Branche erst lernen muss zu stellen: Worin liegt der tatsächliche Wert dessen, was wir tun, unabhängig davon, wie lange es dauert?

Trust is the invisible foundation of every agency relationship. Until at some point the question hangs in the room: is the agency actually working for me? Or only for itself?  
The problem rarely begins with a major rupture. It begins with small irritations. The promised consultant disappears. Decisions become opaque. The work can hardly be assessed. Both sides notice that something isn’t right. And still don’t talk about it.  
Here, two parties try to find out why trust has turned into caution. And whether a relationship can be repaired whose biggest problem is that both sides pretended for too long that everything was fine.  
Minutes of a therapy session.  

#### Opening

The therapist asks both parties to introduce each other as they perceive each other.  
K. begins: “An agency. We’ve been working together for three years. In the pitch it was love at first sight. Since then I’ve sometimes wondered whether I hired the same agency I met back then.”  
A. nods. Says nothing. The therapist notes: recognition without discussion.  

#### The core problem

The therapist asks K. when trust was first broken.  
K. reflects. “Not broken. Torn. Very slowly. The first tear was when I noticed that the consultant I had signed the contract with suddenly only showed up at big meetings. The actual work came from someone else. Younger. Good, but not what I had expected.”  
A. clears their throat. “We have a team. No one person can do everything. Everyone knows that.” K.: “Known, yes. Discussed, no.”  
The therapist notes: the problem isn’t reality. It’s the silence about it. In 1976, Jensen and Meckling described this phenomenon as moral hazard: when the client can’t fully observe the resources actually being used, structural room opens up for discrepancies between what was promised and what is delivered. Not fraud. But a gap that costs trust.  

#### The structure of the problem

The therapist explains what she has observed in many similar sessions. There is a fundamental imbalance: one side knows more than the other. The agency knows who is really working, how much effort is really involved, what is really possible. The client knows none of this and often can’t reliably judge it even after the project is finished. This is the structural feature of creative services as credence goods: services whose quality can’t be clearly determined even after delivery.  
K. nods slowly. “After every project I feel I have no way of checking whether I was well advised.” A., quietly: “Neither do we, always.” A brief silence.  
The therapist notes: both are caught in the same dilemma. Only one side knows it.

The therapist asks A. why this isn’t communicated openly.  
A. thinks. “Because you don’t sell uncertainty. Clients want trust. Clarity. The feeling of being in good hands.”  
K.: “I want a good feeling, yes. But that also comes from facts. Who is working on my project? With what experience? Why this approach and not another? What did you discard before presenting this to me?” A. is silent.  
K., more calmly: “I’m not asking this to control you. I’m asking because I want to trust your work. And sometimes trust needs insight to create understanding.”  
The therapist notes: transparency is feared as a weakness. Yet it is the only foundation for strength. In 1994, Morgan and Hunt showed that trust in business relationships works as a functional substitute for formal control, but only when it rests on real knowledge, not on a carefully tended impression.  

#### The question of responsibility

The therapist asks who would like to see the problem solved. Both raise their hands at the same time. Then they look at each other. For the first time in this session.  
A.: “We never learned to discuss this openly. The pitch is designed to persuade. For trust through impression. Not for trust through insight.”  
K.: “And I never asked. Because I didn’t know I was allowed to. Because the dynamic was always: you’re the experts. I trust you.” A.: “That’s a high level of trust.” K.: “It was a blind one.”  
The therapist notes: the trust problem is not one side’s failure. It’s the result of a dynamic that both sides failed to question for years.

The therapist closes the session with an assessment.  
Trust between agencies and clients isn’t destroyed. It is structurally weakened. By an information gap that both sides know about and that neither side can close alone. The solution doesn’t lie in better promises. It lies in fewer promises and more insight. In conversations that don’t begin only once something has gone wrong. In a relationship that can bear uncertainty, because it rests on real knowledge, not on a carefully tended impression.  
K. asks whether a second session is necessary. The therapist smiles. “That depends on whether you continue the conversation after this session.”  
End of minutes. Homework for both: say what has so far gone unsaid.

#### State of research

The principal-agent problem, theoretically grounded above all by Ross (1973) and by Jensen and Meckling (1976), describes situations in which a client (principal) hires a service provider (agent) without being able to fully observe how they proceed. The resulting moral hazard risk, with the agent acting in their own interest rather than the client’s, is particularly pronounced in agency practice, because creative services as credence goods (Darby & Karni, 1973) are hard to assess even ex post. The seniority asymmetry between pitch and delivery, with experienced consultants winning the job and less experienced teams delivering it, is a classic case of this moral hazard. The commitment-trust theory of Morgan and Hunt (1994) describes trust as a central driver of successful business relationships and as a functional substitute for formal control: where quality can’t be measured, trust replaces control and thereby becomes a strategically critical resource. An international industry survey (Double Check, 2024) quantifies the resulting trust deficit: only 47% of clients credit their agency with a consistent focus on results, only 40% see it as a long-term growth partner. The master’s thesis (Walter, 2026) shows empirically that high staff turnover at agencies makes this problem worse: every change costs implicit brand and industry knowledge and extends the onboarding period by an average of a quarter of a year.

Das Principal-Agent-Problem, maßgeblich durch Ross (1973) sowie Jensen und Meckling (1976) theoretisch fundiert, beschreibt Situationen, in denen ein Auftraggeber (Prinzipal) einen Dienstleister (Agent) beauftragt, ohne dessen Vorgehen vollständig beobachten zu können. Brands will soon need a second communication strategy. For algorithms.  
Communication has always had a clear target group: people. Everything agencies and brands have done, from campaigns to messages to channel strategies, was aimed at human attention, emotion and decision-making. This basic assumption is starting to tip.  
When consumers increasingly delegate their purchasing decisions to AI assistants, the logic of visibility changes fundamentally. Whoever is invisible to algorithms doesn’t exist, no matter how strong the campaign, no matter how high the budget.  
What follows is not a warning. It is a user manual. For brands that want to understand what it means when the most important target group of the coming years is no longer human.  
Important notice before first use  
This document is aimed at brands that want to remain visible in a world where consumers increasingly delegate purchasing decisions to AI assistants. A study by ARD Media (Heßler, 2026) documents that autonomous software agents are already a reality as intermediaries between brands and end consumers. Please read the following sections in full before you consider your current communication strategy sufficient.  

#### Section 1: Understand the operating principle before you begin.

Algorithms work differently from people. That sounds trivial, but it isn’t when you see how few brands have drawn the consequences so far.  
A person decides emotionally, contextually, sometimes irrationally. They remember a campaign that moved them. They follow a recommendation, they buy because a package appealed to them. An AI assistant does none of this. It evaluates sources by consistency, structure and authority. It cites what is machine-readable. It recommends what is marked as reliable in its training data. The most emotional campaign in the world leaves no impression on it if the brand architecture behind it isn’t right.  
This is not a creativity problem. It is an infrastructure problem.  

#### Section 2: Visibility to algorithms is not a question of budget. It’s a question of structure.

Anyone who believes that more media budget also means more visibility to AI systems hasn’t understood the operating principle.  
Generative Engine Optimization (GEO for short) is the discipline that describes how brands become discoverable and recommendable for AI systems. Aggarwal and colleagues defined the concept in an ACM study in 2024: digital presences must be structured as machine-readable sources of authority so that AI systems classify them as relevant and cite them. GEO thus follows a different logic from classic search engine optimization. It’s not about keywords and click rates, but about whether a brand is recognizable as an authority. An algorithm asked which running shoe it recommends doesn’t fall back on the most beautiful ad. It falls back on what is described most consistently and credibly in its sources.  

#### Warning: Creative excellence alone is no protection.

Many brands are creatively strong and structurally invisible. Campaigns that move people leave no traces in machine-readable systems unless they are backed by consistent, structured information. Both are necessary. Just one of them is no longer enough.  

#### Section 3: Address two target groups without neglecting either.

The most common wrong reaction to this insight is the assumption that you have to choose: either emotional communication for people or structured communication for algorithms. This assumption is wrong.  
The brands that will win in the long term have understood that both are expressions of the same strategic clarity. A brand that really knows what it stands for (what it solves, why it’s better, who confirms it…) can translate that into an emotional campaign and at the same time into a machine-readable architecture. The starting point is the same. The translation is different. Whoever doesn’t have this starting point fails with both target groups at once.  

#### Warning: Inconsistency is punished.

AI systems are sensitive to contradictions. A brand that claims one thing on its website and another in trade articles loses algorithmic credibility. People forgive inconsistency. Algorithms ignore what they can’t classify.  

#### Section 4: Frequently asked questions

Isn’t this just SEO under a new name? No. Classic search engine optimization aims at ranking in results lists. GEO aims at being cited and recommended by generative AI systems as a reliable source. The mechanisms are related, but not identical. Anyone treating GEO as SEO 2.0 will be turning the wrong dials.  
Do we have to rewrite our entire communication strategy? No, but add to it. The point is to add a layer that has been missing so far: the machine-readable translation of what a brand stands for. GEO doesn’t appear in any classic agency value chain. It is a new field of competence that agencies have to develop.  
How long does it take for results to become visible? Longer than with classic campaigns. Algorithmic authority doesn’t come from one-off measures, but from consistent signals over time. That is uncomfortable in an industry used to quick results. It is the truth all the same.  

#### Section 5: Common user errors

Error 1: The campaign is finished. So is the strategy. A campaign is a moment. Algorithmic visibility is a state that has to be maintained continuously. Whoever stops after the launch loses what they have built.  
Error 2: We’re well known. So we’ll be found. Awareness among people and visibility to algorithms are two different things. Many of the world’s best-known brands are algorithmically weak because they never learned to make their strength machine-readable.  
Error 3: This is an IT problem. GEO is not a technical discipline. It’s a strategic one. The question doesn’t begin in the IT department. It begins with a brand knowing what it stands for.  
Error 4: We’ll wait until the market has stabilized. The market isn’t stabilizing. It keeps evolving. Whoever waits until the rules are fixed will find that others have already been playing by them.  

#### Section 6: Getting started

Don’t begin with the technology. Begin with the question of what your brand is. Clearly enough to explain it to an algorithm. If that answer is hard to give, it isn’t a GEO problem. It’s a strategy problem that becomes a GEO problem. And that’s the good news: it can be solved. But it has to be solved before a machine decides whether your brand exists.

Wichtiger Hinweis vor Inbetriebnahme  
Dieses Dokument richtet sich an Marken, die in einer Welt sichtbar bleiben wollen, in der Konsumenten Kaufentscheidungen zunehmend an KI-Assistenten delegieren. #### Abschnitt 1: Funktionsprinzip verstehen, bevor Sie beginnen.

Algorithmen funktionieren anders als Menschen. Das klingt trivial – ist es aber nicht, wenn man sieht, wie wenige Marken bisher die Konsequenzen daraus gezogen haben.  
Ein Mensch entscheidet emotional, kontextuell, manchmal irrational. Er erinnert sich an eine Kampagne, die ihn bewegt hat. Er folgt einer Empfehlung, er kauft, weil eine Verpackung ihn angesprochen hat. Ein KI-Assistent tut nichts davon. Er bewertet Quellen nach Konsistenz, Struktur und Autorität. Er zitiert, was maschinenlesbar ist. Er empfiehlt, was in seinen Trainingsdaten als verlässlich markiert wurde. Die emotionalste Kampagne der Welt hinterlässt bei ihm keinen Eindruck, wenn die dahinterstehende Markenarchitektur nicht stimmt.  
Das ist kein Problem der Kreativität. Es ist ein Problem der Infrastruktur.  

#### Abschnitt 2: Sichtbarkeit für Algorithmen ist keine Frage des Budgets. Es ist eine Frage der Struktur.

Wer glaubt, mit mehr Mediabudget auch für KI-Systeme sichtbarer zu werden, hat das Funktionsprinzip nicht verstanden.  
Generative Engine Optimization (kurz GEO) ist die Disziplin, die beschreibt, wie Marken für KI-Systeme auffindbar und empfehlenswert werden. Aggarwal und Kollegen definierten das Konzept 2024 in einer ACM-Studie: Digitale Auftritte müssen als maschinenlesbare Autoritätsquellen strukturiert werden, damit KI-Systeme sie als relevant klassifizieren und zitieren. GEO folgt damit einer anderen Logik als klassische Suchmaschinenoptimierung. Es geht nicht um Keywords und Klickraten, sondern darum, ob eine Marke als Autorität erkennbar ist. Ein Algorithmus, der gefragt wird, welchen Laufschuh er empfiehlt, greift nicht auf die schönste Anzeige zurück. Er greift auf das, was in seinen Quellen am konsistentesten und glaubwürdigsten beschrieben wurde.  

#### Warnhinweis: Kreative Exzellenz allein ist kein Schutz.

Viele Marken sind kreativ stark und strukturell unsichtbar. Kampagnen, die Menschen bewegen, hinterlassen in maschinenlesbaren Systemen keine Spuren – wenn sie nicht mit konsistenten, strukturierten Informationen unterlegt sind. Beides ist notwendig. Nur eines davon reicht nicht mehr.  

#### Abschnitt 3: Zwei Zielgruppen ansprechen, ohne eine zu vernachlässigen.

Die häufigste Fehlreaktion auf diese Erkenntnis ist die Annahme, man müsse sich entscheiden: entweder emotionale Kommunikation für Menschen oder strukturierte Kommunikation für Algorithmen. Diese Annahme ist falsch.  
Die Marken, die langfristig gewinnen werden, haben verstanden, dass beides Ausdruck derselben strategischen Klarheit ist. Eine Marke, die wirklich weiß, wofür sie steht (was sie löst, warum sie besser ist, wer das bestätigt…) kann das in eine emotionale Kampagne übersetzen und gleichzeitig in eine maschinenlesbare Architektur. Der Ausgangspunkt ist derselbe. Die Übersetzung ist unterschiedlich. Wer diesen Ausgangspunkt nicht hat, scheitert an beiden Zielgruppen gleichzeitig.  

#### Warnhinweis: Inkonsistenz wird bestraft.

KI-Systeme sind sensibel für Widersprüche. Eine Marke, die auf ihrer Website anderes behauptet als in Fachartikeln, verliert algorithmische Glaubwürdigkeit. Menschen verzeihen Inkonsistenz. Algorithmen ignorieren, was sie nicht einordnen können.  

#### Abschnitt 4: Häufig gestellte Fragen

Ist das nicht einfach SEO unter einem neuen Namen? Nein. Klassische Suchmaschinenoptimierung zielt auf Ranking in Ergebnislisten. GEO zielt darauf, von generativen KI-Systemen als verlässliche Quelle zitiert und empfohlen zu werden. Die Mechanismen sind verwandt, aber nicht identisch. Wer GEO als SEO 2.0 behandelt, wird die falschen Stellschrauben drehen.  
Müssen wir unsere gesamte Kommunikationsstrategie umschreiben? Nein, aber ergänzen. Es geht darum, eine Ebene hinzuzufügen, die bisher gefehlt hat: die maschinenlesbare Übersetzung dessen, wofür eine Marke steht. GEO taucht in keiner klassischen Agentur-Wertschöpfungskette auf. Es ist ein neues Kompetenzfeld, das Agenturen entwickeln müssen.  
Wie lange dauert es, bis Ergebnisse sichtbar werden? Länger als bei klassischen Kampagnen. Algorithmische Autorität entsteht nicht durch einmalige Maßnahmen, sondern durch konsistente Signale über Zeit. Das ist unbequem in einer Branche, die an schnelle Resultate gewöhnt ist. Es ist trotzdem die Wahrheit.  

#### Abschnitt 5: Häufige Anwenderfehler

Fehler 1: Die Kampagne ist fertig. Damit auch die Strategie. Eine Kampagne ist ein Moment. Algorithmische Sichtbarkeit ist ein Zustand, der kontinuierlich gepflegt werden muss. Wer nach dem Launch aufhört, verliert, was er aufgebaut hat.  
Fehler 2: Wir sind bekannt. Also werden wir auch gefunden. Bekanntheit bei Menschen und Sichtbarkeit für Algorithmen sind zwei verschiedene Größen. Viele der bekanntesten Marken der Welt sind algorithmisch schwach positioniert, weil sie nie gelernt haben, ihre Stärke maschinenlesbar zu machen.

Beginnen Sie nicht mit der Technologie. Beginnen Sie mit der Frage, was Ihre Marke ist. Klar genug, um es einem Algorithmus zu erklären. Wenn diese Antwort schwerfällt, ist das kein GEO-Problem. # Seite: What Corporate Film Can Do That No Campaign Can

### What Corporate Film Can Do That No Campaign Can

#### The attention paradox

We live in the age of the three-second hook. Every brand wants to stop the scroll. Every video needs a punchline before anyone has time to look away. The solution? Make it shorter. Make it louder. Add another cut. Another twist. Another reason not to leave.  
But there is a problem with this logic: the more we compete for attention with constant stimulation, the less attention we seem to have left.  
And somehow, brands have decided that the answer is even more of the same.  
Short formats are not the enemy. They are useful, efficient and often entertaining. But they have a limitation: while they can create awareness they rarely create meaning. You can show what a company does in 15 seconds. You cannot explain why it matters.  
Attention is not the same as connection. And a view is not a relationship.  
In short  
We have learned to capture attention. We are forgetting how to hold it.  

#### People still have time. Just not for everything.

The idea that nobody has time for long content is simply wrong. People spend hours watching Twitch streams, listening to podcasts or finishing entire seasons in one weekend.  
The numbers tell a similar story. In its 2024 Connected Audience Study, Involved Media found that 93% of US respondents had watched long-form video in the previous month, spending an average of 34 hours per week across long-form video platforms. In 2026, Edison Research reported that 82% of weekly podcast consumers actively watched video podcasts.  
People are not running out of time. They are becoming more selective about what deserves it.  
The difference is simple: nobody watches a ten-hour stream because it is short. They stay because something keeps happening. Because they care about the person, the story or the world they are entering.  

#### The documentary advantage

A campaign is built to deliver a message. A documentary is built to make you care.  
That is a fundamental difference. Campaigns compress. Documentaries explore. Campaigns tell you what to think about a brand. Documentaries give you reasons to form your own opinion.  
A well-made corporate documentary does not need to shout its message. It lets people, places, conflicts and real moments do the talking. It can show the complexity behind a product, the ambition behind an idea or the people behind a promise.  
It replaces claims with evidence. Instead of saying “we are innovative”, it shows what innovation actually costs, who makes it happen and what is at stake.  
This is not a longer commercial. It is a different kind of relationship with an audience.  

#### The conditions for staying

Of course, length alone is not a virtue. Nobody owes a brand 30 minutes of their life.  
Long-form branded content only works when it meets a few conditions:  
A story, not a sales pitch. There must be a genuine question, tension or discovery. Something that makes people want to know what happens next.  
Real people, not brand characters. Audiences connect with human complexity, not perfectly rehearsed corporate statements.  
Access, not advertising. Show us something we would not normally get to see. Open doors. Reveal processes. Let us into a world.  
A point of view. Neutrality is not the same as honesty. A film needs a perspective, even if it does not have all the answers.  
Respect for the audience. No endless product shots. No forced brand moments. No explaining what the audience can understand for itself.  
A 2025 study by Krowinska and Dineva in the Journal of Marketing Management reinforces a related point: meaningful engagement with branded content depends on functional, emotional and symbolic value. Being branded is not enough. There has to be something in it for the viewer.  

#### Who should make long films?

Not every brand needs a documentary. But some have much more to gain from one than from another campaign.  
Brands with complex products or processes that need explaining. Companies whose value lies in expertise, craftsmanship or people rather than a simple product benefit. Businesses operating in industries where trust matters more than impulse. And brands with a genuine cultural perspective, a strong mission or a world that people would find interesting even without the logo.  
Especially when a brand wants to change how it is perceived, not just remind people that it exists.  

#### The bottom line

Short formats are good at getting people through the door. Long formats can give them a reason to stay.  
In a world where everyone is fighting for the next three seconds, choosing to tell a 30-minute story is almost an act of rebellion. It signals confidence. Confidence in your subject, your people and your audience.  
The future of brand communication will not be short or long. It will be about knowing when to do which. But brands that only know how to interrupt will eventually struggle to mean anything.  
In short  
Campaigns buy attention. Documentaries earn it. And the brands worth spending time with have nothing to fear from taking their time.

Wir leben im Zeitalter des Drei-Sekunden-Hooks. Jede Marke will den Scroll stoppen. Jedes Video braucht eine Pointe, bevor jemand Zeit hat wegzuschauen. Die Lösung? Kürzer machen. Lauter machen. Noch ein Schnitt. Noch ein Twist. Noch ein Grund, nicht zu gehen.

Eine Kampagne ist gebaut, um eine Botschaft zu vermitteln. Eine Dokumentation ist gebaut, damit dir etwas wichtig wird.  
Das ist ein grundlegender Unterschied. Kampagnen verdichten. Dokumentationen erkunden. Kampagnen sagen dir, was du über eine Marke denken sollst. Dokumentationen geben dir Gründe, dir deine eigene Meinung zu bilden.

Das ist kein längerer Werbespot. Das ist eine andere Art von Beziehung zum Publikum.

Natürlich ist Länge allein keine Tugend. Niemand schuldet einer Marke 30 Minuten seines Lebens.  
Longform-Branded-Content funktioniert nur, wenn er ein paar Bedingungen erfüllt:  
Eine Geschichte, kein Verkaufsgespräch. Es braucht eine echte Frage, eine Spannung oder eine Entdeckung. Etwas, bei dem man wissen will, wie es weitergeht.  
Echte Menschen, keine Markenfiguren. Das Publikum verbindet sich mit menschlicher Komplexität, nicht mit perfekt einstudierten Unternehmensstatements.  
Zugang, keine Werbung. Zeig uns etwas, das wir normalerweise nicht zu sehen bekommen. Öffne Türen. Leg Prozesse offen. Lass uns in eine Welt hinein.  
Eine Haltung. Neutralität ist nicht dasselbe wie Ehrlichkeit. Ein Film braucht eine Perspektive, auch wenn er nicht alle Antworten hat.  
Respekt vor dem Publikum. Keine endlosen Produktshots. Keine erzwungenen Markenmomente. Nichts erklären, was das Publikum selbst verstehen kann.  
Eine Studie von Krowinska und Dineva im Journal of Marketing Management aus dem Jahr 2025 untermauert einen verwandten Punkt: Echtes Engagement mit Branded Content hängt von funktionalem, emotionalem und symbolischem Wert ab. Gebrandet zu sein reicht nicht. Für die Zuschauerinnen und Zuschauer muss etwas drin sein.  

#### Wer sollte lange Filme machen?

Nicht jede Marke braucht eine Dokumentation. Aber manche können mit einer deutlich mehr gewinnen als mit einer weiteren Kampagne.  
Marken mit komplexen Produkten oder Prozessen, die erklärt werden müssen. Unternehmen, deren Wert in Expertise, Handwerk oder Menschen liegt statt in einem einfachen Produktnutzen. Firmen in Branchen, in denen Vertrauen mehr zählt als der Impuls. Und Marken mit einer echten kulturellen Perspektive, einer starken Mission oder einer Welt, die Menschen auch ohne Logo interessant fänden.  
Vor allem dann, wenn eine Marke verändern will, wie sie wahrgenommen wird, und nicht nur daran erinnern, dass es sie gibt.  

#### Unterm Strich

Kurze Formate sind gut darin, Menschen zur Tür hereinzuholen. Lange Formate können ihnen einen Grund geben zu bleiben.  
In einer Welt, in der alle um die nächsten drei Sekunden kämpfen, ist die Entscheidung für eine 30-minütige Geschichte fast ein Akt der Rebellion. Sie signalisiert Selbstvertrauen. Vertrauen in dein Thema, deine Menschen und dein Publikum.  
Die Zukunft der Markenkommunikation wird weder kurz noch lang sein. Es wird darum gehen, zu wissen, wann was passt. Doch Marken, die nur unterbrechen können, werden es irgendwann schwer haben, überhaupt etwas zu bedeuten.  
In short  
Kampagnen kaufen Aufmerksamkeit. Dokumentationen verdienen sie sich. Und Marken, mit denen man gern Zeit verbringt, müssen keine Angst davor haben, sich Zeit zu nehmen.

# Seite: The Human Is Not the Magic Ingredient

**SEO-Beschreibung:** We build memorable brands that spark excitement and ignite engagement.

Take · 4 min read  

### The Human Is Not the Magic Ingredient

Home

#### The comforting story

Whenever someone asks what AI means for creativity, the industry has a reassuring answer: The human will remain important. Because humans have intuition. Emotion. Empathy. Originality. Things machines cannot do.  
Sounds good. But it is not a strategy. It is wishful thinking.  
If AI can produce a hundred campaign ideas before a creative team has finished its first coffee, being slightly more creative is not enough. And being slower certainly isn’t a competitive advantage.  
The human will not remain relevant simply because we say so. Nor because human creativity is somehow sacred.  
The human remains valuable when human judgment changes the outcome. And when agencies can show clients exactly why that change matters.  
In short  
Human creativity is not a birthright. It is a job that needs to be done.  

#### The value of disagreement

AI is very good at producing plausible answers. That is both its strength and its weakness. A plausible idea looks finished. It sounds convincing. It fits the brief. And suddenly, nobody asks whether it is actually any good.  
This is where human judgment becomes valuable: In challenging the version everyone already likes.  
A 2025 study at the CHI Conference tested 52 pairs of people working on creative tasks, with and without ChatGPT. AI support did not improve overall performance. In fact, participants developed their ideas less. The tool had made it easier to produce answers, not necessarily to think them through.  

#### So what should agencies do?

Make disagreement part of the process. Every AI-assisted concept should come with a counterargument: What is generic about it? Where does it misunderstand the brand? What cultural assumption does it get wrong?  
And protect the person making that argument. A good objection should count as good creative work, even when the team eventually chooses the AI suggestion.  
For important decisions, test both routes against the same criteria: brand fit, distinctiveness and audience response. Show clients what the AI suggested, why the team challenged it and what the alternative achieves.  
Do not sell human creativity as an abstract promise. Sell the quality of the decision.  

#### The value of responsibility

There is another problem. AI makes producing more options incredibly cheap. Which means options are no longer the scarce resource. Decisions are.  
If an agency presents 50 variations, it has not necessarily done more creative work. It may simply have outsourced the difficult part to the client.  
The real work starts before the prompt. It starts with understanding the world the brand operates in. Talking to customers. Visiting places. Listening to people who are not in the meeting. Finding out what the brief leaves out.  
Then comes the uncomfortable part: taking a position.  
Before designing anything, write down one sentence: We want to say X, not Y. AI can explore the possibilities, but it should not decide what the brand stands for.  
Research supports this more balanced view. A 2025 meta-analysis by Holzner, Maier and Feuerriegel, covering 28 studies and more than 8,200 participants, found that people working with generative AI performed better on creative tasks on average. But their ideas also became significantly less diverse.  
The lesson is not to use less AI. It is to use it without surrendering the choices that make the work distinctive.  
And the job does not end at delivery. Agencies should define what success looks like, check what the work actually does and take responsibility when it misses the mark.  

#### What needs to change

This requires more than a new prompt workshop.  
Agencies need to budget for research, critical review and creative judgment. They need to present fewer, better-reasoned options instead of selling volume. They need to make the human contribution visible in proposals and charge for it.  
Clients need to stop buying creative output by the kilo. A pitch should not only reward the best-looking execution, but also the strongest reasoning behind it.  
And the industry needs to talk about the alternatives that were rejected, the assumptions that were challenged and the mistakes that were made. Not just the shiny success stories.  

#### The bottom line

If agencies only deliver images, headlines and variations, they are competing with AI on its strongest ground. And they will lose.  
The human advantage is not that we can generate something machines cannot. It is that we can question what should be generated in the first place, connect it to reality and take responsibility for what happens next.  
But that advantage only exists if we build a process around it. And if clients are willing to pay for it.  
In short  
AI can make the work. Humans must make the decisions. But only if we give them the right to disagree – and the responsibility to be right.

#### Was sich ändern muss

Dafür braucht es mehr als einen neuen Prompt-Workshop.  
Agenturen müssen Budget für Research, kritische Prüfung und kreatives Urteil einplanen. Sie müssen weniger, aber besser begründete Optionen präsentieren, statt Masse zu verkaufen. Sie müssen den menschlichen Beitrag in Angeboten sichtbar machen und dafür Geld verlangen.

Und die Branche muss über die verworfenen Alternativen sprechen, über die hinterfragten Annahmen und die gemachten Fehler. Nicht nur über die glänzenden Erfolgsgeschichten.  

#### Unterm Strich

Wenn Agenturen nur Bilder, Headlines und Varianten liefern, konkurrieren sie mit KI auf deren stärkstem Terrain. Und sie werden verlieren.  
Der menschliche Vorteil ist nicht, dass wir etwas erzeugen können, was Maschinen nicht können. Er besteht darin, dass wir infrage stellen können, was überhaupt erzeugt werden sollte, es mit der Wirklichkeit verbinden und Verantwortung für das übernehmen, was danach passiert.

#### The promise

Ever more companies are building in-house creative teams, and the pitch is simple. With AI, anyone in marketing can generate copy, images and campaign ideas in seconds. Faster than an agency. Cheaper than an agency. It sounds like logic. But most of all: It is a trap.  
Speed is not excellence. Companies that treat creativity as a solvable production problem are missing an important point.  
Creativity was never a production problem. It is a perspective problem.  
In short  
AI fixes how fast you make things. It does not fix what you can’t see.  

#### Why experts get stuck

Insiders know everything: the product, the politics, the history of every best practice. That’s their value. And also their limit. Ask a team that has worked on one product for ten years for a fresh idea, and you get the eleventh version of the old one. None of them is lazy. Our brains are efficient: they take the path they know. Over time, the company’s language becomes the company’s restriction.  
Scott Page and Lu Hong made this case in their famous 2004 model: diverse problem solvers can beat groups of high-ability ones. The math has been contested, but the intuition holds. A team that thinks alike misses alike.  

#### The multiplier

Now add artificial intelligence. A language model returns the statistically likely answer, built from the past. A team with blind spots that prompts a model gets its own assumptions back, only faster, varied and more polished. What you ask depends on what you already believe is possible. The model answers inside that frame and hands it back as insight.  
The evidence is piling up. In a 2024 Science Advances experiment, about 300 people wrote short stories, some with AI-generated ideas. Outside raters found the AI-assisted stories more creative. But those stories were about 10 percent more similar to each other. Better for each writer, worse for everyone. A 2025 study led by Liwei Jiang found the same across models: ask different AI systems an open-ended question and they answer in strikingly similar ways. The authors call it the “Artificial Hivemind”.  
Your competitors use the same models. Welcome to the Sea of Sameness. You are paying to join it.  
In short  
AI does not remove blind spots. It scales them, and everyone gets the same ones.  

#### The new job of the outsider

If average becomes free, average stops being worth anything. What stays scarce is a different way of looking. Agencies were once paid for hands. AI now does much of the handwork. What it cannot do is stand outside.  
A good external team brings patterns from other industries. It asks naive questions: why does everyone in your category do the same thing? It has no stake in internal politics. Not necessarily better tools. But a different position.  

#### The bottom line

Mediocrity just got cheaper. Distinctiveness did not. Insourcing with AI optimizes the status quo. That is fine for work that should be fast and cheap. But if you want to enter new markets and stand out, you don’t need faster yes-sayers. You need friction. The question is not “agency or AI?” It is: who will show us what we can no longer see?  
In short  
Cheap average is everywhere. The outside view is rare.

AI isn’t destroying the agency. It’s destroying the middle.  
There is a comfortable story the industry is telling itself right now: AI is a tool. We use it. We become more efficient. And in the end we are stronger than before. The story isn’t wrong. But it is incomplete.  
Because AI doesn’t only change how agencies work. It changes which agencies are still needed at all. What is repetitive disappears. What has no clear competence loses its reason to exist. What remains are the ends of the spectrum: the specialists and the networks. The middle in between is shrinking. Not dramatically. Not overnight. But structurally and unstoppably. The middle in between is shrinking. Not dramatically. Not overnight. But structurally and unstoppably.  

#### Whoever is there for everyone is indispensable to no one.

For decades, the agency landscape settled comfortably in the middle. Full service wasn’t a promise, it was an avoidance strategy. Whoever offers everything never has to choose. Whoever never chooses never has to explain themselves. That worked as long as clients were willing to pay for convenience. That time is over. The GWA Frühjahrsmonitor 2026 documents a growing divide between agencies that turn change to their advantage and those that don’t.  

#### Efficiency is not a differentiator, it’s the price of admission.

Generative AI has lowered the production costs of standard services to a level that would have been unthinkable just five years ago. Text variants, format adaptations, simple visual worlds: tools do all of this faster and cheaper than any team. Williamson’s transaction cost theory describes this mechanism precisely: when the costs of internal coordination fall through technology, the make-or-buy boundary shifts to the disadvantage of the external agency. What is repetitive and standardizable moves back into the hierarchy. Or goes to platforms. Agencies that have defined their value through these services are losing the basis for their prices. Not because they have become worse. But because the market price for what they do is tending toward zero.  

#### Specialization is not a limitation. It’s the only protection.

What can’t be standardized can’t be automated. Deep industry knowledge built up over years. A signature that is unmistakable. The ability to see things in a particular context that others overlook. Barney’s resource-based view (1991) describes exactly these qualities as sustainable competitive advantages: valuable, rare, hard to imitate, non-substitutable. The boutique that is better than everyone else at exactly one thing. And sleeps more soundly than the agency that is good at many things.  

#### Size only protects those who are truly big.

On the other side of the spectrum are the networks. Global coordination, international campaigns, proprietary technologies. These are services that small agencies structurally cannot provide. According to the World Federation of Advertisers, two thirds of multinational companies now have their own in-house agency units. An increase of 16 percentage points since 2020. This development hits the middle harder than the extremes: whoever has neither the scalability of a network nor the depth of a specialist agency loses on both sides at once.  

#### The middle is not a business model. It’s a postponement.

What is happening right now is not a crisis. It’s a correction. The market is sorting itself along a logic that has always held true, but was long covered up by growth and convenience. The empirical findings of the master’s thesis confirm this from the client’s perspective: not one of the decision-makers surveyed still believes in the agency for everything. Whoever can’t clearly name their value loses it. Not to AI. Not to the competition. But to their own indecision.  

#### The question is not whether you position yourself. The question is whether you do it in time.

There are agencies that have already made this decision. They know what they can do, what they can’t do and what they will never try. They have stopped joining every pitch and started turning down the right ones. These agencies are growing. Not because the market has become easier, but because clarity in a moment of confusion is the strongest competitive advantage you can have.  

#### State of research

The transaction cost theory of Coase (1937) and Williamson (1985) provides the economic explanation for the current market shakeout: companies outsource services when external coordination costs are lower than internal ones. When these costs fall through digitization and AI-supported automation, the make-or-buy boundary shifts to the disadvantage of the external agency. This mechanism explains structurally why in-housing of operational standard services is not a passing trend but an economic consequence. Barney’s resource-based view (1991) describes which agencies survive nonetheless: those offering resources that can’t be replicated internally, being valuable, rare, hard to imitate and non-substitutable. This applies to highly specialized boutiques and to integrated networks, but not to a middle without a clear unique selling point.
